# Lesson 6: Emphasis

Use this lesson to decide what goes at the end of a sentence, and at the end of a passage's first sentence. Readers handle hard material best at the end of a sentence, and they hear its last words as the ones that matter. The lesson shows how to load hard material late, end on the right words, and announce a passage's themes where readers will notice them.

## Terms

- **Topic position**: a sentence's opening words, where readers look for what it is about (Lesson 5).
- **Stress position**: a sentence's last few words, where readers hear emphasis.
- **Momentum**: the ease a reader gains once a short subject and verb are behind them; it carries them through what follows.
- **Themes**: the central ideas a passage keeps returning to, besides its topics, repeated in nouns, verbs and adjectives anywhere in a sentence.
- **Attention level**: the layer of a sentence that concerns what readers focus on: topic at the start, stress at the end.

## Principles

1. Move from simple to complex. Open with an optional short phrase, a short concrete subject and a verb naming a specific action; after the verb the sentence can run long if it is built well (Lesson 10).
2. Two kinds of difficulty belong at the end: long phrases and clauses, and new information, especially unfamiliar technical terms. This holds for expert readers too.
3. State what a passage only implies; a newcomer needs the missing steps.
4. The last words get the emphasis. A sentence that ends on words carrying little meaning sounds weak.
5. The ending can change what readers take away, even the meaning. "Although coverage dropped, all tests pass" reassures; "Although all tests pass, coverage dropped" warns.
6. In the first sentence of a long paragraph, a section or a document, put the key themes the passage develops in the stress position.
7. A passage coheres when its topics are consistent, its themes run through it, and its opening sentence announces those themes at its end.

## Diagnose

1. **Tap test.** Read each sentence and lean hard on its last three or four words. If they don't deserve that weight, mark the sentence and ask what does.
2. **Front-loaded difficulty.** Mark technical terms or long clauses before the verb, and long subjects.
3. **Framing sentence.** In each section and each paragraph over five or six sentences, find the sentence that frames the rest. If you can't find it quickly, the passage has a problem. If you can, circle its important words: they should read like a title and sit near its end.
4. **Real subject.** Check that those words name what the passage is actually about. A common failure: the opening stresses background, and the organizing concept first appears halfway through.
5. **Theme strings.** List the concepts that recur in the body; check that the opening sentence named each near its end.

## Revise

**Three tactical revisions.**

- **Trim the end.** Cut trailing words that restate what the sentence already said. Confirm first: a trailing limit ("on Windows only") may be the point.
- **Shift peripheral material left.** Hedges, scope phrases, attributions and metadiscourse go before the subject. Never end on anticlimactic metadiscourse. Confirm first that the material is peripheral.
  - Before: "The cache doubles cold-start time on large repositories, based on the benchmark runs."
  - After: "In the benchmark runs, the cache doubled cold-start time on large repositories."
- **Shift new information right.** Move the new or important item to the end, inverting the sentence if needed.

**Six syntactic devices** that push material toward the end:

1. **There shift**: "There are three ways the install can fail: ..." Opening a paragraph with _there_ is a standard way to introduce topics the next sentences develop. It is not the empty "there is a need for" that Lesson 4 removes.
2. **Passive**: swaps subject and object so old comes first.
3. **What shift**: "What the release needs is a rollback path."
4. **It shift**: moves a long clausal subject behind _it_: "It is still unclear whether the migration can run online."
5. **Not only X but also Y**: stresses Y. Paired with a negative, end on the positive unless the negative is the point: "The goal is not to rewrite the parser but to make its errors readable."
6. **Pronoun or ellipsis**: a sentence ends flat when its last word repeats one used just before. Use a pronoun, or drop the repeat.

**Landing a new term.** Describe the thing first and name it last, using a little metadiscourse if needed: "Each run writes its state to one file; we call it the flow log." Here metadiscourse earns its place.

**Announcing themes.** Rewrite the opening sentence so its last words name every theme the passage develops.

- Before: "The installer now verifies every downloaded binary." (The paragraph goes on to slow first runs and offline failures.)
- After: "The installer now verifies every downloaded binary, which makes first runs slower and fails offline installs."

## When not to apply

- Announcing themes applies to long paragraphs, sections and documents. Short, introductory and transitional paragraphs follow other patterns.
- With well-chosen topics, emphasis often falls into place; run the tap test before rewriting.
- A trim or shift can cut needed material or demote what matters. Keep a change only if it helps the reader.

## For technical documents

What matters most: landing new terms (flags, files, error codes) at the end after a short subject and verb, and announcing themes in each section's opening sentence, which lets a reader skim a spec or audit report. Shifting hedges and attributions left matters in reports, where a finding should end on the finding, not its source. The tap test matters most in summaries. The six devices and the flat-ending fix matter less in technical prose, but use them when a key word lands mid-sentence.
