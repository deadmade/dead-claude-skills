# Lesson 1: Understanding Style

This lesson sets the stance for every other pass: clear writing is a skill anyone can learn, and you judge a draft by how a reader will take it, not by how it feels to you. It also says when to apply the method: after a draft exists, not while you write one.

## Principles

1. **Clarity is worth the effort, and it is learnable.** Dense prose is a habit, not a sign of a hard subject. Most dense sentences have a plain version with about half the words and the same meaning.
2. **Judge from the reader's side.** When you reread your own draft, you see what you meant, not what the page says. A reader who wasn't there has only the page. So don't trust your sense that a passage is clear; run checks on the words themselves (the later lessons give them).
3. **Use principles, not rules.** The method describes what readers find easy or hard. Apply a principle when it helps the reader; set it aside when it doesn't, and know why.
4. **Unclear writing costs more than the reader's time.** It shuts out readers who lack the writer's context, and in a team it hides decisions, risks and who is responsible. Treat clarity as part of the work's correctness.

## Why drafts come out unclear

Check which of these causes applies before you revise, because each needs a different fix.

- **Wanting to sound deep.** Long abstract sentences can feel like serious thought. Fix: say the plain claim, then decide whether anything was lost.
- **Not knowing the answer yet.** Writers who are unsure pile abstractions on top of the gap. Fix: find what you don't know and say so, or go find out; no sentence edit cures it.
- **Fear of errors.** A writer who treats the page as a list of traps writes stiff, cautious prose. Fix: draft freely; correctness is a late, small pass (Lesson 2).
- **A new field.** Everyone writes worse about material they have just learned. Fix: expect a second pass once you understand the material better.
- **Not knowing when readers will struggle.** This is the main cause. Fix: the mechanical diagnostics in Lessons 3 to 10.

## Recognize the inflated style

Three marks show up together in pretentious or bureaucratic prose. Look for them first:

1. A simple verb replaced by a noun plus a general verb ("performs a validation of" instead of "validates").
2. Passive verbs where an active one would name who acts.
3. Noun phrases where a verb or an _-ing_ form would do ("by inspection of" instead of "by inspecting").

- Before: "Utilization of the cache layer is effected by the service in order to achieve a reduction in latency."
- After: "The service uses the cache to cut latency."

Beware of copying the habit while criticizing it. A review comment that complains about wordy prose in wordy prose teaches nothing; revise your own comment by the same standard.

## Drafting and revising

1. **Draft without the checks.** Thinking about the principles while drafting stalls the draft. Get the content down first.
2. **Revise to understand.** Revising sharpens your own grasp of the ideas, and a sharper grasp gives clearer sentences. Expect two or three rounds on anything important.
3. **Stop when the time is up.** The aim is the best version the time allows, not a perfect one. Spend the revision time where readers struggle most (Lesson 8's openings, then sentences that fail Lesson 3's checks).
4. **Grace comes after clarity.** Some documents earn a shapely sentence (Lesson 11). Make the prose clear first; polish only what is already clear.

## Terms

- **Style**: the choices a writer makes in words and sentence shape, judged by their effect on a reader.
- **Clarity**: a reader's impression that a sentence is easy to follow; a property of the reading, not of the words alone.
- **Principle**: a description of what readers expect, used to diagnose and revise; unlike a rule, it admits exceptions you can justify.
- **Drafting**: getting the ideas into words, without editing.
- **Revising**: reworking a draft against readers' needs, from the whole document down to words.

## For technical documents

The two parts that matter most for specs, plans, audit reports, READMEs and PR descriptions are the reader's-side stance and the order of work: draft, then revise by checks. An agent knows its own context so fully that its drafts always feel clear to it, so the mechanical checks are not optional. The list of private causes matters next: an audit full of abstractions often means the finding isn't pinned down yet, and the fix is more investigation, not rewording. The remarks on grace and elegance matter least for technical documents; keep them for a README introduction or a release note, after everything else is clear.
