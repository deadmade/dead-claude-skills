# Lesson 11: Elegance

This lesson covers the devices that make a clear sentence memorable: balance, a strong ending, and controlled rhythm. They are optional and come last; a sentence must be clear and coherent before it can be elegant. Use them sparingly, at the places a reader should remember.

## Terms

- **Balance**: two or more parts of a sentence that echo each other in structure, sound, rhythm or meaning.
- **Coordinated balance**: balance between parts joined by _and, or, nor, but, yet_.
- **Noncoordinated balance**: balance between parts of different grammatical roles, such as subject and object, or a subordinate clause and the main clause.
- **Climactic emphasis**: ending a sentence on its heaviest, most important words.
- **Echoing salience**: a stressed word at the end that repeats the sound or meaning of an earlier one.
- **Chiasmus**: two balanced parts where the second reverses the order of the first (A B, B A).
- **Suspension**: delaying the main clause behind parallel openers so the ending lands harder (the periodic sentence of Lesson 10).

## Principles

1. Elegance grows out of clarity; it never replaces it. The five clarity principles stay in force: characters as subjects, actions as verbs, old before new, short before long, topic then stress.
2. Elegance is technique and taste, not rules. The devices below can be learned; judging when they serve and when they inflate takes practice.
3. Restraint is part of the craft. A device's effect shrinks the more often it appears. When in doubt, the plainer sentence wins.

## Device 1: balance

1. Find a sentence that makes a comparison, a contrast or a pairing.
2. Give the paired parts the same structure, and where you can, matching sound or meaning: verb against verb, object against object, modifier against modifier.
3. Make each later balanced element a little longer than the one before it (short to long).
4. Balance can also cross grammatical roles: a subject against an object, a relative clause's predicate against the main predicate, a direct object against a prepositional object, a subordinate clause against the main clause.
5. Use a balance frame to generate thought: start "X is not only A but also…" and ask what the second half must be. The pattern can surface an idea you hadn't written.

Before: "The old installer was slow, and users found it unpredictable when upgrades happened."
After: "The old installer made every install slow and every upgrade a gamble."

## Device 2: climactic emphasis

End the sentence on strength. Five ways, from plainest to most marked:

1. **A weighty word, or a pair of them.** Light words (prepositions, pronouns) end weakly; adjectives and adverbs are heavier; nouns heavier still, and nominalizations heaviest. A nominalization hurts as a subject but satisfies at the end, especially two in balance. Replace a phrasal verb that strands its preposition with a one-word verb ("the change we signed off on" → "the change we approved").
2. **_Of_ plus a weighty word.** A closing "of the …" phrase quickens the rhythm just before the final stress ("the cost of every retry" versus "every retry's cost").
3. **Echoing salience.** End on a word that repeats or answers the sound or sense of an earlier one.
4. **Chiasmus.** Reverse the order in the second half: "We test what we ship, and we ship only what we test."
5. **Suspension.** Stack parallel _if_ or _when_ clauses before the main clause so the conclusion arrives with force. This overrides the Lesson 10 advice to open with the point; reserve it for a closing sentence.

Before: "The migration plan is something that the database team still has concerns about."
After: "The database team still doubts the safety of the migration."

## Combining devices

Ambitious passages stack them: coordinated series, resumptive modifiers, stressed nominalizations and balanced pairs at sentence ends. Stacked devices signal deliberate craft, so the combination fits a conclusion or a statement of purpose, not routine prose.

## Length and rhythm

1. Unplanned length is fine unless every sentence is under about fifteen words, or every one far longer.
2. Vary length on purpose: a short sentence after long ones closes a thought; long ones carry a build-up.
3. Read the passage aloud: a run of same-length sentences drones.

## Checking an elegant sentence against the clarity principles

When a sentence seems elegant, confirm it still passes:

1. Its subjects are main characters.
2. Its key actions are verbs.
3. It opens on known information and ends on new.
4. A short segment opens and frames longer ones; within coordinations, shorter comes first.
5. It opens on its topic and stresses its last words.

A deliberate departure (a delayed subject, a long subject before a short complement) is fine when something compensates, such as a heavy ending.

## How to develop it

1. Notice which devices writers you admire use.
2. Imitate a balanced model's pattern on your own subject, not its words.
3. Practice finishing a sentence's first half with a balancing second half.
4. Practice moving weak endings to nominalizations and _of_ phrases.
5. Compare a passage before and after with a reader; ask what changed.
6. Ask of the result: elegant, or just inflated? When unsure, cut words.

Elegant prose thus joins three things that seem to conflict: simple subjects and verbs, complex balance and rhythm, and stressed endings.

## For technical documents

Most of this lesson matters little in specs, plans and audit reports: their reader wants the claim, and ornament draws attention to the writer. SKILL.md leaves this lesson out of its standard for that reason. Three parts still help: end sentences on the weighty, new term (the stress principle of Lesson 6); keep paired items parallel and short to long, which is also a clarity rule; and vary sentence length so a short sentence marks a verdict. Balance, chiasmus and suspension fit a README's opening, a design rationale's conclusion, or a PR description's one-line summary, used once. Never trade precision for rhythm.
