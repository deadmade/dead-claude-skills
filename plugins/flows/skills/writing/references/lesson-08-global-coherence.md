# Lesson 8: Global Coherence

Use this lesson on the structure of a document and its sections. Readers build coherence from signals on the page: an opening that frames each unit, a point before the detail, recurring key terms, and a visible order. The lesson shows how to put those signals in on purpose.

## Terms

- **Global coherence**: the sense that a whole document or section hangs together (Lessons 5 and 6 cover passages).
- **Opening segment** and **body**: the short start of any unit, and the longer part it frames.
- **Point sentence**: the sentence stating a unit's point and naming the concepts the body develops; in a paragraph, the topic sentence.
- **Themes**: the key concepts a point sentence announces and the body repeats.
- **Relevance**: the visible link between each sentence or part and the point of its unit.

## Principles

For the whole document:

1. Readers must see where the introduction ends and each section begins and ends. Give each section a heading that carries its key themes. If the genre has no headings, draft with them and remove them at the end.
2. The point comes at the end of the introduction and names the themes the rest develops. If you have good reason to hold it for the conclusion, end the introduction with a sentence that promises it and still names the themes.
3. The body repeats those themes regularly.

For each section and subsection, the same again:

4. Open with a short segment.
5. End it with a sentence stating the section's point and themes.
6. Repeat those themes through the section.

Two more requirements:

7. **Relevance.** Every sentence must visibly serve its unit's point by doing one of these: give background or context, state the point of the whole or a section, give a reason, give evidence for a reason, explain reasoning or method, or consider another view. A sentence that does none reads as incoherent.
8. **Order.** Readers look for the principle behind the order of parts:
   - **Chronological**: earlier to later, or cause to effect. Signal with first, then, finally, as a result.
   - **Coordinate**: parallel parts that equally support one claim, ordered by something readers can follow, such as importance or complexity. Signal with first, second, also, more important.
   - **Logical**: example and generalization, premise and conclusion, claim and counterclaim, either way round. Signal with for example, it follows that, on the other hand.

The general principle under most lessons: a unit reads clearly when a short, easy segment frames the longer, harder one after it. That segment is a simple sentence's subject; a complex sentence's main clause, stating the point before its support; a paragraph's first sentence or two; a section's first paragraph or more; a document's introduction, which may run pages but stays much shorter than the body.

## Diagnose

1. **Outline.** Read only the title, the headings and each unit's point sentence. Together they should form the argument.
2. **Opening segments.** In each section and long paragraph, draw a line after the opening segment and circle its words that name the themes of what follows. If you can't, revise.
3. **Point position.** The point sentence should close the opening, however long, so it is the last thing read before the body.
4. **Themes.** Check that the circled themes recur in the body.
5. **Relevance.** Name the kind of relevance each sentence serves; mark the ones that serve none.
6. **Order.** Name each section's ordering principle. Mark any section that follows how you did the work.

## Revise

- **Point buried at the end**: make it the last sentence of the opening, with its themes near the end of that sentence.
  - Before: "We ran the suite on three runners, timed each stage, then profiled the slowest. Dependency install took most of the time. The cache key changed on every run because it hashed the lockfile's mtime. So most CI time goes to reinstalling dependencies."
  - After: "Most CI time goes to reinstalling dependencies, because the cache key changes on every run. The key hashes the lockfile's mtime, not its contents. Timing each stage on three runners showed dependency install as the slowest; profiling it showed a cache miss every time."
- **Irrelevant sentences**: cut or move them.
- **Discovery order**: turn a narrative of the work into a coordinate or logical order. One way: list each paragraph's point on its own line, shuffle the lines, have a fresh reader or agent order them, and restructure to match.
- **Hidden order**: add its signal words.
- **Fear that an early point spoils the document** is unfounded: a reader motivated by the problem (Lesson 7) reads on.

## When not to apply

- For paragraphs the pattern is guidance, not a template; many work without it. A paragraph with its point at the end does little harm in a well-framed document. Unframed paragraphs in an unframed document leave readers lost.
- Paragraph length follows the genre's conventions.
- A reflective essay for readers who want to follow the writer's thinking can ignore these patterns. For readers who must understand and act, follow them: it may feel formulaic to you, but readers attend to substance.

## Where to spend revision time

On beginnings, in this order: the introduction, major sections' openings, subsections and long paragraphs, then sentence openings. When reading a hard document, mark its problem, main claim and each section's point, read only those, then read in full.

## For technical documents

Nearly all of this matters most: point-first sections, headings that carry key terms, the outline test and the relevance check are what make a spec, plan or audit report skimmable. Rebuilding discovery order into a logical one is the most common fix in agent-written reports, but chronological order stays right for steps a reader follows, such as an install procedure or a migration plan. The paragraph-level pattern matters less: apply it to long paragraphs only. Removing headings rarely applies, since technical documents keep them.
